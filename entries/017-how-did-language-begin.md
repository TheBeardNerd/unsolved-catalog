Herodotus tells of an Egyptian pharaoh who had two infants raised in silence, waiting to hear what language would come out of them untaught; the experiment was flawed but the curiosity was ancient even then. By 1866 the speculation had grown so unmoored that the Linguistic Society of Paris wrote a ban on the topic into its founding bylaws: no papers on the origin of language. The ban expressed a real problem. Speech leaves no fossils, and the question sat officially disreputable for a century before science found ways back in.

## Why it matters

Language is the technology underneath every other human technology. It is how knowledge outlives its owner, how strangers coordinate, how this catalog exists. Every human society has a full language and no other species has anything close, which makes its origin the origin of the human difference itself. Explaining how it arose would connect genetics, anatomy, archaeology, and cognition into a single story about what happened in our lineage and when.

## What has been tried

The indirect evidence has accumulated impressively. Anatomy: the vocal tract, breathing control, and hearing range of modern humans are tuned for speech in ways our relatives' are not. Genetics: a family with a mutated FOXP2 gene showed specific speech and language deficits, revealing one gene in the machinery, though emphatically not a language gene. Comparative work: apes can learn hundreds of signs but not syntax, while songbirds learn their songs in ways that echo how children learn sound systems. Theorists divide over whether language began as gesture and moved to the mouth, or was vocal from the start; whether it emerged gradually from a protolanguage of words without grammar, or arrived relatively suddenly; and whether it evolved for communication or first for thought.

## Where the edge is

There is no consensus on when language emerged. Estimates run from a few hundred thousand years ago, with the appearance of our species or earlier, down to arguments that fully modern language is younger than that. Whether Neanderthals had it remains genuinely open. The field has data now, and multiple live theories, but nothing yet that forces agreement.

## What would count as an answer

Convergence: genetic changes dated to a period, anatomical readiness in the same fossils, and archaeological traces of symbol use lining up on one window and one sequence. No single discovery can settle this question, which is precisely what makes it hard.
