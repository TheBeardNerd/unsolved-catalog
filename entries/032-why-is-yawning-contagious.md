There is a fair chance this page makes you yawn. Reading about yawning does it, watching it does it, even hearing it does it, and the effect crosses species lines: dogs catch yawns from their owners. The contagion is so reliable that researchers use written passages like this one as an experimental trigger. Behind the party trick sit two nested unknowns. Science cannot yet say what yawning itself is for, and it cannot say why this particular behavior, almost alone among behaviors, spreads on sight.

## Why it matters

Yawning is ancient equipment: fish do it, birds do it, fetuses do it in the womb. Anything preserved that widely for hundreds of millions of years is presumably doing work, and not knowing the work is a real gap in physiology. The contagion question reaches further. Catching a yawn is one of the simplest cases of one nervous system involuntarily echoing another, which makes it a clean model for studying how brains mirror brains, the machinery suspected to underlie imitation, synchrony, and perhaps empathy. A solved yawn would be a foothold on much larger cliffs.

## What has been tried

The folk theory, that yawning raises blood oxygen, was tested directly in the 1980s: breathing high oxygen or high carbon dioxide changes breathing but not yawning. It is the rare hypothesis in this catalog that is simply dead. The current leader for yawning's function is thermoregulation, the deep breath and stretch cooling the brain; yawning rises in warm conditions within limits, and cooling the head suppresses it, though critics find the evidence circumstantial. Rival accounts cast yawning as a state-change signal, marking transitions between sleep and wakefulness, or as an arousal reset. On contagion, the fashionable link was empathy: contagious yawning reportedly varying with social closeness and empathic traits. Replication has been unkind, with larger studies finding weak or no association, and the tidy story of yawns as an empathy meter has loosened. What survives is the raw phenomenon: robust, involuntary, present in some social species and apparently absent in others.

## Where the edge is

Function and contagion may not even share an explanation, and the field lacks agreement on either. Whether contagious yawning synchronizes group vigilance or sleep timing, piggybacks on imitation circuitry, or serves no function at all remains open.

## What would count as an answer

For yawning, a manipulation of its proposed job with measurable consequences when yawns are prevented. For contagion, identification of the triggering circuit and a demonstrated benefit of the spread, or good evidence that it is a functionless echo. This entry may take two retirements.
