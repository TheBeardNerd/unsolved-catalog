It arrives without warning: an ordinary moment abruptly wearing the costume of a memory, complete and detailed and impossible, and alongside it the certain knowledge that it is wrong. That double awareness makes déjà vu one of the strangest common experiences a mind produces, a hallucination of familiarity accompanied by its own fact-check. About two thirds of people report it. The term was proposed in 1876, and a century and a half later the mechanism remains unconfirmed, largely because the experience is brief, spontaneous, and gone before any instrument is watching.

## Why it matters

Déjà vu is a rare crack of light into how recognition works. The brain evidently computes the feeling of familiarity separately from the retrieval of an actual memory, since déjà vu is that feeling firing alone, and it comes with a built-in error detector that usually wins. Mapping the glitch would map three systems at once: familiarity, recollection, and the monitor that arbitrates between them. The monitor may matter most. Its failures are implicated in confabulation and in the unshakeable false convictions of some psychiatric and dementia conditions; déjà vu is the benign case where the alarm works.

## What has been tried

The strongest biological clue comes from epilepsy: seizures beginning in the medial temporal lobe are often announced by intense déjà vu, and directly stimulating structures there, particularly the rhinal cortices that compute familiarity, can produce the experience on demand. That establishes where the feeling can be generated, though not why healthy brains occasionally fire it. For the healthy case, laboratory work has made progress by engineering the illusion: scenes constructed in virtual reality to share spatial layout with previously seen scenes, without being recognizably similar, reliably elicit déjà-vu-like reports, supporting a familiarity-without-source account. The same experiments punctured a folk belief, finding that the accompanying sense of knowing what happens next predicts nothing better than chance. Older theories, from split-second double perception to Freudian wish traces, survive mainly as history.

## Where the edge is

Whether everyday déjà vu is a miniature version of the epileptic discharge, a retrieval near-miss, or the error monitor catching a routine familiarity misfire is unresolved, and the experience's decline with age, opposite to what a failing-memory story predicts, fits no theory cleanly.

## What would count as an answer

Catching one in the act: neural recordings during spontaneous déjà vu in a healthy brain, showing which systems misfired and which caught it. Until then, the feeling remains what it has always been, a memory of nothing.
